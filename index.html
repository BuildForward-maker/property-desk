2. Your budget buys a very good resale flat, or a mediocre new one

A 10-to-15-year-old tier-1 flat

Prestige, Brigade, Sobha and Puravankara buildings of this vintage in established addresses trade below ₹10,000/sqft. You get 1,150–1,400 sqft, mature trees and a working association, for the price of 850 sqft of new build.

A 4-to-8-year-old tier-2 gated community

Modern layouts, working amenities, no structural questions, ₹9,000–11,000/sqft in Sarjapur Road, Panathur, Hoodi and Electronic City. The largest pool of genuinely suitable stock for you.

A new compact 2BHK on the periphery

Budigere Cross, Kanakapura Road, Jigani, Hosa Road. Everything new, nothing finished. Fine to live in, weaker if it must rent from day one.

My read: 5 to 12 years old, tier-1 or strong tier-2, in a community of 200+ units. More carpet area than a new build, a locality that already functions, a rental market that already exists, and enough comparable transactions that you can price it honestly.

Grade-A launches in Bengaluru now cluster on 2029–2031 completion. Short-term letting is legal in Bengaluru and needs no trade licence. Vocabulary you will hear this month

Khata, A and B

A municipal tax record, not a title document. A-khata means regularised with BBMP. B-khata means deviations — restricted lending, harder resale, and it should trade 40–50% below comparable A-khata. If a seller says it converts "soon", price it as permanent.

e-khata / e-Aasthi

The digitised khata now needed for registration. One unpaid property tax quarter blocks it automatically.

Occupancy Certificate

Certifies the building is legally habitable. Mandatory post-2014. No OC means no individual khata, and 5% GST still applies on a new purchase.

Encumbrance Certificate

Registered transactions against the property — mortgages, charges, transfers. From Kaveri Online. Pull it again 48 hours before registration.

Guidance value

The state's minimum valuation. Stamp duty is charged on the higher of guidance value or your price. In many pockets it sits below market, which is why registered transaction data reads low.

Super built-up vs carpet

Almost every Bengaluru price is per square foot of super built-up, including your share of lobbies, lifts and clubhouse. Carpet is 60–70% of it. Always ask which basis a rate uses.

UDS, undivided share of land

Your proportional ownership of the land under the building. It decides everything if the building is redeveloped, and almost nobody checks it. Ask for it in square feet.

BBMP, BDA, BMRDA, panchayat

Four approving authorities across the metro. Panchayat-approved peripheral layouts carry more regularisation risk than BBMP ones. Ask which one approved the layout.

Traps that catch first-time buyers here specifically

  • Lake and storm-water buffer zones. A-khata confirms building plan compliance, not environmental compliance. Properties near Varthur and parts of Whitefield have received demolition orders while holding valid A-khata.
  • Water source. Many peripheral projects run on borewells plus tankers, not Cauvery. Ask the summer tanker spend per flat per month — it can be ₹2,000–5,000 and it is not in the listing.
  • Unallotted parking. Confirm your slot is identified, and whether it is deeded or merely allotted by the association.
  • Common areas never handed over. Common in 5-to-10-year-old projects. Ask whether builder handover is complete and whether litigation is pending about it.
  • Unregistered associations. Ask to see the registration certificate, not a WhatsApp group.
  • Maintenance arrears follow the flat, not the seller. Make a no-dues certificate a condition of registration.

What to look for in each shot when you review them later

  • Ceiling corners and wall bases. Brown or grey blooming means water has been there. Fresh paint in one patch means it was there recently.
  • Cracks. Hairline in plaster is cosmetic. Diagonal cracks at beam-column junctions, or cracks that continue across two surfaces, are not.
  • Window and balcony junctions. Most Bengaluru seepage starts here, not at the roof.
  • Bathroom walls shared with a bedroom. The commonest failure point in flats over eight years old.
  • Balconies that have been enclosed. Extremely common, and a deviation from the sanctioned plan that can block khata transfer.
  • The building exterior from three sides. Streaking below windows, spalling concrete or exposed rebar tells you about the association's maintenance discipline.
  • The basement. Standing water, efflorescence on walls, or a sump that looks recently pumped.
  • Lit windows after dark. Occupancy rate tells you about resale liquidity better than any broker will.

When it comes down to two or three, put them in columns. Differences that felt small on separate days become obvious side by side.

Resale is where your budget works, and where the risk lives. This is how to price, check and negotiate one.

Age is the biggest lever on price

0–3

Nearly new

Full modern amenities, smallest carpet area for the money, highest price per square foot. Builder warranty may still run; snags may still be unresolved. You pay a premium that starts depreciating immediately.

4–8

The comfortable zone

Modern layouts, amenities working, association formed, snags fixed, no structural questions. Typically 10–18% below new. Lowest-risk band for a first-time buyer.

9–15

Where the value is

Tier-1 stock in prime addresses below ₹10,000/sqft. Bigger carpet area than anything new. Lifts, plumbing and wiring approach their first replacement cycle — factor a sinking-fund contribution.

16–25

Proceed carefully

Cheap per square foot, often the best locations. Expect major common-area capex, dated electricals, thin covered parking. Get a structural engineer. Resale liquidity narrows.

25+

A land bet, not a home bet

You are buying undivided share of land and a redevelopment option, not a building. Only sensible on a large plot in a prime location with redevelopment already in discussion. Not a first purchase.

My pick: 5 to 12 years, tier-1 or strong tier-2, community of 200+ units. Scale matters — larger communities have better-funded associations, more comparable transactions to price against, and far easier resale when you exit.

What a resale flat should cost

What this flat is worth
Roughly 1.6% structural depreciation per year for fifteen years, tapering after, then adjusted for floor, condition, amenities and paperwork. A sanity check, not a valuation. Anchor the final number on registered transactions in the same society from Kaveri Online.

What to check on a resale that you would not on a new build

The building

  • Seepage stains at ceiling corners, around windows, in bathroom-adjacent walls. Visit after rain if you can.
  • Hairline plaster cracks are cosmetic; diagonal cracks at beam-column junctions are not. Structural engineer on anything over ten years old — it costs a few thousand rupees.
  • Lift age and service records. A lift replacement is a six-figure special levy per flat.
  • Water tanks, pumps, and whether the sewage treatment plant actually runs.
  • External paint cycle — when it was last done and who paid.

The association and the money

  • Last two years of accounts. No sinking fund means you get levied for the first big repair.
  • Any special levy declared or under discussion. The question sellers most often dodge.
  • Maintenance arrears on this flat — they follow the flat. Get a no-dues certificate.
  • Whether the builder handed over common areas and corpus, and any litigation about it.
  • Owner-occupier versus tenant ratio. Heavily tenanted communities maintain worse and resell slower.
  • The short-let clause, if you intend to Airbnb.

Resale-specific paperwork

  • The full title chain, not just the seller's sale deed. Back at least thirty years through the mother deed. One unsigned legal heir makes ownership disputable.
  • If the seller has a loan, their bank holds the originals. Structure release and payment on the same day, and take the originals at registration.
  • If the seller is an NRI, TDS jumps above 20% and you are liable for deducting it correctly. Get a chartered accountant involved before you sign.
  • Joint or inherited ownership means every owner and heir signs, or gives a registered power of attorney. Verify the POA is registered and current.
  • Check the sanctioned plan against the flat. Enclosed balconies and merged units are common and can block e-khata.

The 100-year lease: this does not apply to Bengaluru apartments

There is no lease clock counting down on a Bengaluru flat. Private builder apartments in Bengaluru — Prestige, Sobha, Brigade, Godrej, Puravankara and the rest — are sold freehold. You own your flat and a permanent, undivided share of the land under it, with no expiry, no ground rent and no renewal. A flat that is fifteen years old does not have "eighty-five years left". It has no clock at all.

Leasehold does exist in India, but it is a different animal: land leased for 30 to 99 years by a development authority, most commonly DDA in Delhi, or MHADA and CIDCO in Maharashtra. In Karnataka it shows up mainly on government-allotted housing, some older BDA lease-cum-sale sites, cantonment land and defence land. It is the exception here, not the rule.

What you should actually check is one line in the sale deed and one number:

  • Is the title freehold? Your lawyer confirms this from the deed and the title chain. If anything says lease, ask how many years remain and price it very differently — leasehold with a short tail is genuinely worth less and banks lend against it reluctantly.
  • What is your undivided share of land, in square feet? This is the number almost nobody asks for, and it is the one that matters over decades.

What actually ages: the building, not the land

The right version of the instinct behind the lease question is this. A concrete structure has a functional life of roughly 50 to 80 years, and redevelopment typically becomes the conversation somewhere between 40 and 60. So in a flat you are buying two assets glued together:

The structure — depreciating

Loses value every year. Needs a sinking fund, then major capex, then eventually demolition. This is what the age discount in the valuation model above is pricing.

Your undivided share of land — appreciating, permanently

No expiry. In a city adding three to four lakh residents a year, this is the part that compounds. And when the building is eventually redeveloped, your UDS alone decides the size of your replacement flat, your share of any corpus, and your vote in the negotiation.

This is why a low-rise on a large plot and a 40-storey tower can look identical on price per square foot and be very different assets. Same money, far more land under one of them. It is also the strongest argument for plots over flats if the goal is pure wealth building rather than a home.

The seven questions to ask any sales office

  1. What is the RERA-declared completion date, and has it been revised? Ask them to show you the portal, not the brochure. Revisions are recorded.
  2. Which legal entity signs my agreement? Groups use a special-purpose vehicle per project. The parent brand's record does not automatically belong to the SPV.
  3. Has the Occupancy Certificate been applied for, and when? If not applied for, handover is not months away regardless of how the site looks.
  4. What is the delay compensation clause, and at what rate? Read it in the agreement. Ask what triggers it — completion, OC, or offer of possession.
  5. Show me your last three delivered projects, original versus actual handover dates. Then go visit one and talk to residents.
  6. What is the total cost, itemised? Base, floor rise, PLC, car park, club, corpus, maintenance advance, BESCOM and BWSSB deposits, GST, registration. New projects routinely add ₹3–6 lakh over the headline.
  7. Is the payment schedule construction-linked? Construction-linked protects you. Time-linked or front-loaded transfers risk to you.
The GST timing trap. Register before the Occupancy Certificate and you pay 5% GST with no input credit — about ₹6 lakh on a ₹1.2 Cr flat. Register after OC and you pay none. On a nearing-possession purchase, when you register is worth more than what you negotiate.

Before you count on short-let income

  • Read the bylaws and maintenance agreement. Many communities in the tech corridors bar short-term commercial letting. This single check decides whether the plan works.
  • Ask the secretary in writing. "People do it here" is worth nothing at the next general body meeting.
  • Check IT-zoning restrictions if the project is in a designated IT zone.
  • Deposits are now capped near two months' rent under the Karnataka Rent amendment, with tenancies registered with the Rent Authority within 60 days.
  • Don't underwrite the purchase on short-let income you can't survive losing. Enforcement is light today; the direction is toward tightening.
A middle path worth pricing: furnished long-let to corporate tenants on 11-month agreements. Earns 15–25% above unfurnished, no turnover work, no association risk.

  • Complaints filed against the promoter on the RERA portal, and how they were disposed
    • 
      
  • For resale, the same logic runs backwards. A fifteen-year-old building tells you exactly what that builder's concrete, plumbing and waterproofing were worth. Walk the common areas before you look at the flat.

Eight weeks and three days from today. Paying from your own funds, the middle compresses — you have slack a borrowing buyer doesn't.

An honest note on the returns in your brief

    
You mentioned units bought at ₹1.5–3 Cr now worth ₹10–15 Cr, and cousins turning ₹30–60 lakh into ₹1.5–3 Cr inside a year. Those things do happen. They are also not the base rate, and a strategy built on expecting them will disappoint. Some arithmetic worth having in front of you:

    

      
ClaimImplied annual returnHow it compares

      
₹1.5 Cr to ₹10 Cr in 5 years46% a yearRoughly four times Bengaluru's strongest sustained corridor performance. Possible in a single exceptional project; not repeatable as a plan.

      
₹60 lakh to ₹2 Cr in 1 year233%Almost always a pre-launch allotment flipped before registration, or land, not a completed flat. Different risk entirely, and often illiquid.

      
What Bengaluru apartments actually did8–15% a yearBest corridors ran 12–15%; Whitefield about 8–13%; Devanahalli 12–15%. Over three years, several areas did 100%+ — real, but that is 26% a year, not 46%.

      
Plots and land in growth corridors15–25% a yearGenuinely higher than apartments, and where most of the stories you have heard come from. Also where most of the title risk lives.

    

    
The pattern worth copying is not the multiple, it is the timing. Bengaluru's biggest gains came from buying an area two to three years before confirmed infrastructure landed — metro historically adds 15–25% in the two to three years after a station opens. That is repeatable. Buying after the line opens is paying for it. Anjanapura did 205% over three years and Kundalahalli 151%; both were infrastructure catch-up stories, not luck.

    
The other half of the honest version: apartments are a weaker vehicle for this than land or plots. Your undivided share of land appreciates; the concrete on top of it depreciates. If the goal is genuinely a wealth-building portfolio rather than a home, plotted developments in a funded-infrastructure corridor have historically outperformed flats by a wide margin — with correspondingly harder title diligence.

  


  

    
How to actually use this as an investor

    
    
      
  1. Screen on the sweet-spot table, then throw away anything where the growth story is an announcement rather than a funded project. Metro Phase 3A is announced. The Blue Line is funded and being built. Those are not the same asset.
    2. 
      
  2. Verify the growth number before believing it. Portal YoY figures are asking-price movements and are noisy — some of the 25–57% figures on this page are almost certainly mix effects, not real appreciation. Kaveri registered transactions for the same area over two years is the honest check.
    3. 
      
  3. Buy where rent is rising faster than price. That is the signal real demand has arrived and price has not caught up. When price rises faster than rent, you are looking at speculation, and yields compress.
    4. 
      
  4. Watch supply. Devanahalli has 15,000+ planned units. Great for choice, hard on price growth. An area with demand and constrained supply beats an area with demand and unlimited land.
    5. 
      
  5. Set an exit before you enter. Which corridor, what holding period, what triggers a sale. Most people never do this and end up holding emotionally.
